\section{Motivation and Main Ideas}\label{motivation-and-main-ideas}

A fundamental hurdle in medium-frequency systematic trading is the
balance between arrival cost and immediacy. Submitting a very large
marketable order leads to significant price impact as it takes liquidity
from the limit order book. On the other hand, splitting a parent order
into smaller chunks over seconds or minutes exposes child fills to
adverse price drifts, and when done naively also leads to significant
price impact. The goal of this work is to reduce the total arrival cost
of a parent order by intelligently calibrating the aggression of child
orders to take advantage of favorable price movements while avoiding
unfavorable ones.

\subsection{Underperformance of Naive
Splitting}\label{underperformance-of-naive-splitting}

Unfortunately, naively splitting a parent order into \(n\) chunks and
filling them as market orders \(\tau\) time units apart has high impact
and exposes the parent order to significant price drifts. The below
example of splitting a 5000-share order into twenty-five 200-share buy
fills shows the significant impact of repeatedly lifting the ask
overtime. The underperformance of this strategy nessesitates a more
intelligent way to split and fill large orders. \begin{center}

\end{center}

\subsection{Book Imbalance as a Primary
Signal}\label{book-imbalance-as-a-primary-signal}

We chose to use the imbalance in quantity on the top six levels of the
book as our signal to calibrate the aggression of our fill strategy. We
made this choice because OBI is a well understood, easy to implement,
and powerful signal of price direction on the micro-milli second time
scale. To explore OBI's predictive power, we used the markout plot
below. Within five separate ranges of OBI values, we sample 100,000
timestamps and calculate the difference between the initial mid-price
and the mid-price at 10,000 subsequent times, then average over each of
the 100,000 paths. We use a time horizon of \([0,1]\) second, in tens of
microseconds.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{plots_and_data/section_1/markout_symlog.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-markout}Markout Plot}

\end{figure}%

This plot clearly shows that OBI is a powerful, structural predictor of
price direction within a few milliseconds and that this price motion is
structural, indicated by the length of time that the markouts stay
smooth and near-zero variance. Using this predictive power, our fill
strategy can reliably predict positive and negative price drifts and
take advantage of them.

\section{Methodological Choices and
Innovations}\label{methodological-choices-and-innovations}

\subsection{Sourcing Data}\label{sourcing-data}

We utilize the \texttt{MBP-10} schema from Databento, which provides the
top 10 levels of the book for a selection of stocks and date range. The
dataset includes all order book updates (trades, adds, cancels, etc.)
which allows us to reconstruct the book at every book update. The data
is sourced from
\href{https://databento.com/docs/schemas-and-data-formats/mbp-10?historical=python&live=python&reference=python}{Databento}.
We fetch data through a simple \texttt{DatabentoGenericAdapter} class
that uses a hierarchical caching structure. This allows us to call the
Databento API only for new information, which is necessary to manage
storage and memory for L2 book data. The adapter caches on
\texttt{{[}tickers,\ start,\ end,\ dataset,\ schema{]}}. Here, our
schema is always \texttt{MBP-10} and we use the \texttt{XNAS.ITCH}
Dataset which gives us full access to the Nasdaq exchange.

We use L2 data for three tickers, \texttt{AMZN}, \texttt{CSCO}, and
\texttt{DRH} from \texttt{"2026-03-18":"2026-04-18"}. We chose these
tickers because they cover different varieties of equities.
\texttt{AMZN} is a liquid small-tick stock that should offer ample
resilience to price impact and relatively high and volatile bid-ask
spreads. \texttt{CSCO} is a liquid large-tick stock that still has deep
liquidity but larger spreads and more variability throughout the day.
Finally, \texttt{DRH} is an illiquid large-tick stock that offers a
challenge to minimizing costs. It cannot accept large amounts of
liquidity and moves more slowly than \texttt{AMZN} and \texttt{CSCO}.
Put together, this dataset is 28.7GB, on the order of RAM capacity for a
sequential process but requires some management for parallel processing
tasks, like the below.

\subsection{Strategy Evaluation}\label{strategy-evaluation}

To evaluate each of the following strategies, we utilize a parallel
processing trial workflow. At initialization, we set the numerous
required hyperparameters, each listed below.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{Latency\ =\ 100us}, \texttt{Poisson\ Replenishment\ =\ 15.0},
  \texttt{PARENT\_QTY\ =\ 10\_000}, \texttt{MAX\_WORKERS\ =\ 10},
  \texttt{N\_TRIALS\ =\ 5\_000}, \texttt{SEED\ =\ 12345},
  \texttt{HORIZON\ =\ 300s}
\end{itemize}

Then we scan the full dataset's parquet cache and split into one
\texttt{.parquet} for each ticker. We then randomly sample
\texttt{N\_TRIALS\ =\ 5\_000} initial timestamps from the L2 dataset,
ensuring that no two windows from the initial timestamp plus
\texttt{HORIZON\ =\ 300s} seconds overlap more than a few seconds. Then
for each combination of ticker and fill strategy, we initialize
\texttt{MAX\_WORKERS\ =\ 10} CPU workers and scan the relavent ticker
\texttt{.parquet} file for each window and run the fill strategy on that
window only, randomly choosing the side of the order from
\texttt{{[}\textquotesingle{}BUY\textquotesingle{},\ \textquotesingle{}SELL\textquotesingle{}{]}}.
This workflow balances memory requirements and parallelized processing
speed, which brought down the time cost of running trials half an hour
per trial sequentially over the full dataset to 5000 trials in a few
minutes (depending on the ticker).

To measure the performance of each fill strategy, we save information
including \texttt{side}, \texttt{parent\_filled},
\texttt{parent\_arrival\_cost}, \texttt{mean\_child\_arrival\_cost},
\texttt{median\_child\_arrival\_cost}, and \texttt{parent\_lifetime}.
Below we compare our final successful strategy with the naive strategy
using the above metrics, and go over some failed strategies and their
associated learnings along the way. More on specific definitions of the
above metrics can be found in Section~\ref{sec-definitions}.

\subsection{Failure: OBI Aggressive
Only}\label{failure-obi-aggressive-only}

Beyond simply tweaking the benchmark our first true pass at an
improvement used OBI to time our fills using the parameter
\texttt{OBI\_THRESHOLD\ =\ 0.85}. For a buy order, if the OBI was above
the threshold that indicated an impending upward price move, so the
logic was to fill a buy order immediately of size
\(`MAX_SIZE` * |OBI|\), wehre \texttt{MAX\_SIZE\ =\ 350}. This would
dynamically size orders based on confidence about subsequent price
moves, and attempt to only fill at advantageous times. Below is a plot
of this strategy in action.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{plots_and_data/section_2/failed_runs/obi_aggressive.png}

}

\caption{\label{fig-obi}OBI Aggressive Only Fills}

\end{figure}%

This strategy had a few issues. Firstly, calibrating the parameters
above was difficult, and for some time windows, the OBI simply never hit
the threshold it never filled. More crucially though, this method led us
to the final improvement and a successful strategy. This strategy
originated from an \emph{alpha-generation perspective}. Using OBI to
\emph{time} orders is what a strategy would to in order to \emph{enter
positions} and take advantage of future returns. It took some time and
effort to escape this way of thinking and reach an \emph{execution}
based perspective, where a strategy's goal is to \emph{minimize cost} by
setting up a sequence of \emph{fills} (not changing positions) over a
set time horizon. Allowing the price to drift toward and away from the
ideal fill and taking advantage of favorable conditions, not predicting
returns to be captured.

\section{Definitions and Appendix}\label{sec-definitions}

Throughout this work we refer to a few concepts that require specific
definitions. We lay them out below for reference.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  OBI: We define order book imbalnce (OBI) using the top \emph{six}
  levels of the book, as follows:
  \begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-obi}{
  \begin{split}
  OBI = \frac{\Sigma_{i=0}^5 Q_{i}^{bid} - \Sigma_{i=0}^5 Q_{i}^{ask}}{\Sigma_{i=0}^5 [Q_{i}^{bid} + Q_{i}^{ask}]}\in [-1, 1]
  \end{split}
  }\end{equation}
\item
  Price Impact : When referring to price impact we inlcude only the
  mechanical impact of taking liquidity from the limit order book. Below
  is a visualization of the mechanical impact for reference. Our order
  book simulation is discussed in Section~\ref{sec-sim}, and includes

  \end{center}
\item
  Arrival Cost: When referring to arrival cost of a parent or child
  order, we are referring to the volume-weighted average price of a fill
  minus the mid price at the time of execution, divided by mid price and
  converted to bps. For a parent order, the VWAP sums over child order's
  individual vwaps in the same way, and the mid price is set to the
  price at the time the parent order sent to the execution engine. For
  child orders, the mean and median arrival costs are calculated across
  child orders for a given parent. Below, \(q_i\) and \(p_i\) are the
  individual fill's quantities and prices respectively.
  \begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-vwap}{
  \begin{split}
  p_{vwap} &= \frac{\Sigma_{i=0}^nq_i\,p_i}{\Sigma_{i=0}^nq_i}\\ 
  \text{ArrivalCost (bps)} &= \frac{p_{vwap} - p_{mid}}{p_{mid}} * 10^4
  \end{split}
  }\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
